# Pip's Iceberg Office — Presentation Script

A plain-language narration script for walking someone through the app, chapter by
chapter. Written for an audience with **zero coding background** — no technical
words required to understand any of it. Each chapter matches one stop in the app.

How to use this: read the narration out loud (or adapt it to your own voice) while
you click through that chapter's "Before" and "After" scenes on screen. The
**"Show"** line tells you what to click while you talk.

---

## Opening

Welcome to Pip's Iceberg Office. Pip is a penguin who runs an office built into an
iceberg, and every room in this office used to have a small, everyday annoyance —
something clunky, confusing, or just not quite right. This is a tour of ten of
those rooms. In each one, you'll see two versions side by side: how the room
**used to work**, and how it **works now**. Click things in both to see the
difference for yourself.

**Show:** Click "Start the tour."

---

## Chapter 1 — The Iceberg Notice Board

There's a notice board in the front office that tells everyone what's happening
today.

**Before:** any penguin who walks by can walk right up and scribble on the board
directly — cross things out, write over it, mess it up — even though they're not
really the one in charge of it.

**After:** other penguins can no longer touch the board themselves. They can only
send a request asking for it to be changed. Only Pip, the one actually
responsible for the board, is allowed to make the real edit.

**The idea:** it's safer when only the right person can actually make a change,
and everyone else has to ask first instead of just reaching in and editing it
themselves.

**Show:** Click a penguin in the "Before" scene (watch the board get messed up),
then click Reset. Click a penguin in the "After" scene (watch it send a request
instead).

---

## Chapter 2 — Checking on the Chicks

Pip has a nest of four baby chicks he needs to check off as "seen today."

**Before:** he has to tap each of the four chicks one at a time — four separate
taps for four chicks.

**After:** he taps the whole nest once, and all four chicks get checked off
together, in one motion.

**The idea:** if you're checking off a whole group of things at once, it should
only take one action — not one action per item.

**Show:** Tap each chick individually in "Before" and count the taps out loud.
Then tap the whole nest once in "After."

---

## Chapter 3 — Four Booths, One Rule

There are four different booths at the front office — Shipping, Newsletter,
Promo Code, and Account — and each one used to have its own weird, different rule
for when it was allowed to be used.

**Before:** you have to learn each booth's rule separately, one at a time, and
they all look and behave differently.

**After:** all four booths follow the exact same rule shape. Learn how one works,
and you already understand all four.

**The idea:** when everything follows one consistent pattern, it's easier to
learn, trust, and get right.

**Show:** Reveal each booth one at a time in "Before." Then click any single
booth in "After" and watch all four reveal themselves together.

---

## Chapter 4 — The Bouncer Booth

Pip's bouncer checks birthdates at the rope before letting people in.

**Before:** the bouncer only checks one side of things — is this date too
*early*? He never thinks to check if a date is impossibly far in the *future*.
So a ticket dated the year 2099 sails right through, even though that's clearly
wrong.

**After:** the bouncer properly checks both ends — too early, *and* too far in
the future — so only genuinely reasonable dates get let in.

**The idea:** a real check needs to look at both boundaries, not just the one
that's easiest to remember.

**Show:** Test all three dates in "Before" (notice the far-future date wrongly
gets in). Test the same three in "After" (notice it's correctly turned away).

---

## Chapter 5 — The Guestbook Podium

There's a guestbook at a podium where visitors sign their name.

**Before:** the guestbook assumes you're finished writing the moment you pause
your pen for a beat — even if you're just thinking mid-name, it jumps to
conclusions too early.

**After:** the guestbook waits patiently until you actually step back from the
podium before it reacts at all. Pausing while you're still standing there
signing doesn't trigger anything.

**The idea:** sometimes it's better to wait until someone is genuinely done,
instead of guessing based on a short pause.

**Show:** Type a name in "Before" and pause — watch it jump to "checked" right
away. Type the same in "After" and pause just as long — nothing happens until you
click away.

---

## Chapter 6 — The Fish Counter Line

At the counter, two questions get asked: "Is your name spelled right?" (something
that can be answered instantly, just by looking) and "Is there a fish left?"
(something that actually takes a moment to go check in the back).

**Before:** both questions are forced to wait the same amount of time to answer —
even the one that already has an obvious, instant answer.

**After:** the instant question answers immediately. Only the question that
genuinely needs checking makes you wait for it.

**The idea:** don't make someone wait for an answer that's already known.

**Show:** Click "Ask both" in "Before" (notice both questions sit and wait
together). Click "Ask both" in "After" (notice the spelling question answers
instantly while the fish question is still checking).

---

## Chapter 7 — The Seat Re-Check Booth

There's a seat that's already been taken by someone else, in reality — but the
seating chart doesn't know that yet.

**Before:** you try the seat, and it looks like it went fine — the chart still
says "Available," and it never corrects itself. There's no way to ever find out
the truth.

**After:** trying the seat looks the same at first — but there's a small refresh
button. Press it, and it actually re-checks the seat and finally shows the truth
on the chart: "Taken."

**The idea:** sometimes a record doesn't automatically catch up with reality on
its own — you need a way to explicitly tell it "please double-check."

**Show:** Click the seat in "Before" (looks fine, chart never updates). Click the
seat in "After," then press the refresh button and watch the chart correct
itself.

---

## Chapter 8 — The Nurse's Station

A patient's chart lists a few possible problems: too short, left blank, missing a
number, no special character.

**Before:** to find out if one specific problem is present, the nurse has to
read down through the *entire* list, one item at a time, until she happens to
reach the one she's actually looking for.

**After:** she can just directly ask "is this one specific problem present?" and
get the answer immediately — no reading through the rest of the list first.

**The idea:** if you already know exactly what you're looking for, you shouldn't
have to search through everything else to find it.

**Show:** Click "Scan chart" in "Before" and watch it check each item one by one.
Click "Ask directly" in "After" and watch it jump straight to the answer.

---

## Chapter 9 — The Translator Booth

Two departments — an old-fashioned clerk and a newer office — speak different
"stamp languages" when approving paperwork.

**Before:** paperwork stamped the old-fashioned way gets thrown away and ignored
by the newer office. It simply doesn't understand those old stamps at all.

**After:** there's now a translator penguin standing at the wall between them,
who automatically carries the paperwork across and makes sure it's understood on
the other side — without the old clerk having to change how he does his job at
all.

**The idea:** old and new ways of doing things can now understand each other,
without anyone having to redo old work.

**Show:** Click "Send report" in "Before" (watch the paper get dropped and lost).
Click "Send report" in "After" (watch the translator carry it across
successfully).

---

## Chapter 10 — The Universal Adapter Desk

There are three different plug sockets on Pip's desk, and each one used to need
its own completely different, incompatible plug.

**Before:** you need three separate plugs, one for each socket, and you have to
plug each one in individually.

**After:** one single universal adapter fits all three sockets at once. Plug it
in once, and everything lights up together.

**The idea:** one flexible tool that works everywhere beats juggling three
separate ones — and it should be just as easy to undo as it was to set up.

**Show:** Plug in each socket one at a time in "Before." Plug in the single
adapter in "After" and watch all three light up together.

---

## Closing

That's the whole tour — ten small, everyday annoyances around Pip's office, each
one made simpler, faster, more consistent, or more trustworthy. None of them
changed *what* the office does — just made the people (and penguins) doing the
work have a much easier time doing it.

changes listed together.
